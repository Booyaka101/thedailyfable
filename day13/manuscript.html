OUTGOING ### a story in nine messages, found on tape

*Radio drama. One microcassette from an answering machine, Carillon model 58, bought 1994. The machine had a SAVE button. These are the messages somebody chose to keep — and the two the machine kept on its own, after there was nobody left to choose.*

*Total target runtime: ~9–11 minutes with beeps, hiss and air.*

---

PRODUCTION BIBLE

The conceit. Every sound on this tape passed through two bad machines: a telephone line (300–3400 Hz) and a microcassette (wow, flutter, hiss, dropouts). Nothing in the piece may sound better than that. The fidelity ceiling is the fiction.

The wear rule. Tape wear is proportional to REPLAYS, not age. The machine's owner replayed some messages hundreds of times; those are the most degraded. The condition of each message tells you how much it was loved. Wear map (0 = clean, 10 = barely survives):

| # | Message | Year | Wear | Why |
|---|---------|------|------|-----|
| 0 | Outgoing greeting | 1994 | 7 | Played on every call for 16 years |
| 1 | Casserole dish (Marge) | 1994 | 1 | Saved by accident, never replayed |
| 2 | Homesick (Annie) | 1997 | 4 | Replayed sometimes, especially later |
| 3 | Four o'clock (June) | 2001 | 9 | Replayed beyond counting. The worn spot on the tape. |
| 4 | Pick up, Dad (Annie) | 2003 | 2 | Kept, not revisited |
| 5 | The scam | 2006 | 0 | Saved for a reason we learn in message 6 |
| 6 | Fraud dept. follow-up | 2006 | 0 | Saved next to its partner |
| 7 | Sam's dinosaur (Sam) | 2008 | 5 | Replayed often. Grandchildren are replayable. |
| 8 | Disconnection notice | 2010 | 0 | Nobody left to replay it |
| 9 | The last call (Annie) | 2010 | 0 | Recorded once. Never played back. |

The arc. Domestic comedy → distance → the quiet center (June) → grief hiding behind a machine → the world preying on the old → small revenge (Walter fights back, off-tape) → new life (Sam) → the bureaucratic end of a phone line → Annie calling a dead number to hear her father's voice one more time. Close on the greeting itself, degraded, cut mid-word.

Why each message earns its place. Nine saved messages must imply forty years of life. Every message does double duty: what the caller says, and what the saving of it says about the man who pressed SAVE.

Casting. Six voices, all local TTS, all band-limited so far they only need to be *distinct*, not beautiful: WALTER (70s, warm, unhurried), JUNE (70s, dry, quicker), ANNIE (their daughter: 20s in msg 2 → 40s in msg 9; same voice, directed differently), MARGE (neighbor, 60s, broad), SCAM CALLER (male, too smooth), FRAUD OFFICER (female, flat, kind underneath), SAM (Annie's son, 6), ROBOCALL (synthetic — the one voice allowed to sound like TTS, because it is).

Sound design. Beep = 1.4 kHz square-ish blip, twice per message (start/end) as real machines did. Mechanism: motor whir + relay clunk before each playback. Room: none — tape hiss IS the room. Between messages: 1.5–2.5 s of transport noise. No music until the final 90 seconds, and then only a thin, distant piano (the day-12 Salamander, one hand, as if from another room) under the last message and the final greeting playback.

THE TAPE

0 · OUTGOING GREETING — recorded March 1994 — wear 7

*Fumbling. The mic is too close. WALTER has read the manual and is holding it.*

WALTER: Hello. You have reached the home of Walter Hargrove —

JUNE: *(background, not far enough away)* And June.

WALTER: — and June Hargrove, who was supposed to wait for the light.

JUNE: *(closer now, to the mic, sweetly)* And June.

WALTER: We can't come to the phone right now, so kindly leave your name, your number, and a brief message after the tone. After the — *(paper rustle)* — after the beep. It says tone here, but it's a beep.

JUNE: *(background, laughing)* Walter, it's still going —

WALTER: How do you stop the —

*CLICK. BEEP.*

---

1 · MARGE, NEXT DOOR — April 1994 — wear 1

*First real message the machine ever took. MARGE does not trust it and speaks the way people shout at foreigners.*

MARGE: Hello? Hello. This is Marge Kowalski, next door, at number eleven. I am talking to your MACHINE. June, you left the blue casserole dish at bingo Tuesday and Ed put it in our car and never said one word about it, so it's been riding around Cedar Falls for four days. It's on your back step. There's a note under it but the note says what I just said. Also your machine sounds very natural, Walter, very professional. All right. I don't know how to — is it still —

*BEEP.*

---

2 · ANNIE AT COLLEGE — October 1997 — wear 4

*A dorm hallway payphone. ANNIE, nineteen, has rehearsed sounding fine and is doing a C-minus job of it.*

ANNIE: Hi Dad, hi Mom, it's me. It's Annie — Dad, you don't have to write down that I called, I can hear you looking for the pen. Um. Midterms are fine. Iowa City is fine. Everything's fine, that's the report. Um. *(beat, quieter)* It smelled like rain here this morning, and it smelled exactly like the back porch, it was so weird? Like specifically OUR porch. Anyway I have a meal card so nobody has to send anything in a coffee can again, tell Mom. *(beat)* Okay. Call me back Sunday, I'm at the hall phone. Six one nine. You have the number. Okay. Bye. *(doesn't hang up for a second)* Bye.

*BEEP.*

---

3 · JUNE, FOUR O'CLOCK — February 2001 — wear 9

*The worn spot on the tape. Nineteen seconds. JUNE, calling from the clinic, keeping her voice exactly level, which for June is the tell.*

JUNE: Walt, it's me. Don't start the car yet, they're running behind. *(beat)* The results came in and Dr. Prasad wants to go over some things, so — pick me up at four, not three. *(beat)* It's fine. Bring my good coat, it turned cold. *(beat, softer)* Four o'clock. Don't speed.

*BEEP.*

*Production: this message plays back with audible tape damage — dropouts, a pitch sag — and one word ("softer") nearly lost. It is the only message that has clearly been listened to more times than the tape could bear. Nothing in the piece explains it. The wear does.*

---

4 · ANNIE, AFTER — November 2003 — wear 2

*Two and a half years later. ANNIE, calling from her kitchen, tired in the way that has stopped being an event. The machine has become the thing her father hides behind.*

ANNIE: Dad. It's me. Pick up. *(waits — four full seconds of hiss)* I know you're there, the Buick's in the drive, Marge can see it from her window. You can't screen everybody forever, Dad, it's been — *(stops herself)* Thanksgiving is at my place. That's why I'm calling. You don't have to bring anything, you don't have to stay over, Roy will drive you home the same night if that's what you want. Sam's walking now. He walks like you, it's ridiculous, he goes around the coffee table hanging on with one hand like he's inspecting it. *(beat)* Mom would want you at a table, Dad. That's all. Thursday. One o'clock. *(beat)* Pick up the phone next time.

*BEEP.*

---

5 · THE SCAM — March 2006 — wear 0

*Too smooth, too warm, reading a script that has worked on other people's parents.*

SCAM CALLER: Good afternoon, this message is for Mister Walter Hargrove. My name is Kevin Sullivan, calling from the benefits verification department regarding your late wife's account — we show unclaimed funds in the amount of four thousand, two hundred sixty dollars owed to the estate of June Hargrove. Now Walter, to release these funds before the state recovers them, we do need to confirm the account holder's information by end of business today. Call me back on my direct line, that's eight hundred, five five five, zero one — and again, this does concern money owed to your wife, so do please make this a priority. Thank you kindly, and God bless.

*BEEP.*

---

6 · SGT. OKAFOR, FRAUD UNIT — March 2006 — wear 0

*Flat, procedural, and underneath it, genuinely pleased. Saved on the tape immediately after its partner. This pair is the only comedy Walter kept, and it is how we learn what kind of old man he became: the kind who keeps evidence.*

OKAFOR: Mr. Hargrove, this is Sergeant Dee Okafor, Cedar Falls Police, fraud unit, returning yours. Sir, we got the tape. *(the smallest pause)* We've been after this particular operation since Christmas and yours is the first recording where he gives a callback number, so — that was good work, keeping him talking. Off the record, sir, my favorite part is where you ask him to hold on while you find your checkbook and he waits eleven minutes. *(clears throat, procedural again)* We may need a statement. Nothing formal. You have my number. Thank you, Mr. Hargrove.

*BEEP.*

---

7 · SAM'S DINOSAUR — June 2008 — wear 5

*SAM, six, holding the phone the way children do, which is wrong. ANNIE is somewhere behind him. The message he saved and replayed until the tape learned it.*

SAM: Grandpa! Grandpa. It's Sam Hargrove-Miller. *(to Annie, muffled)* He's not — *(back)* Grandpa, okay, so, at the museum today there was a bone of a MOSASAUR which is NOT a dinosaur, it's a lizard, a giant SWIMMING lizard, and the man said they found one in IOWA. In the GROUND. In the OCEAN in the ground, because Iowa used to be an ocean. Grandpa, you were almost alive then! *(ANNIE, background: "Sam!")* Mom says I have to say that's not what she said. Um. Will you take me to the bone museum. Okay bye Grandpa, this is Sam. *(muffled, departing)* He KEEPS a MACHINE that TALKS —

*BEEP.*

---

8 · DISCONNECTION NOTICE — September 2010 — wear 0

*The one voice on the tape that is allowed to sound synthetic. Nobody saved this. The machine simply recorded it, because recording was all it knew how to do.*

ROBOCALL: This is an important message from Heartland Telecom for the account holder at six, four, one... five, five, five... zero, one, one, two. Our records show this account has an outstanding balance of forty-one dollars and sixteen cents. Service to this line is scheduled for disconnection on Tuesday, September twenty-first. To make a payment, or if you believe you have received this message in error, please contact our billing department. Thank you for choosing Heartland Telecom.

*BEEP.*

---

9 · THE LAST CALL — Monday, September 20, 2010 — wear 0

*ANNIE, in Walter's empty kitchen, on her cell phone, calling the house line she is standing next to. The machine's speaker is audible in the room she's calling from — a faint double of everything. She has just heard the greeting (we have not — not yet). The piano begins somewhere here, far off, one hand.*

ANNIE: *(a breath first)* Hi Dad. It's me. *(beat)* It's Annie. *(beat)* They're shutting the line off Tuesday, and the man at the phone company said the — said your greeting goes with it. It's not stored anywhere. It's just here. *(beat)* So I'm in the kitchen. I found the casserole dish, I found your CIVIL WAR magazines, I listened to every message you saved on this thing, Dad, one of them is a TELEMARKETER — *(a laugh that doesn't make it)* — and I know why you kept that one, Sergeant Okafor told me the whole story at the funeral, she stayed forty-five minutes, Dad, she liked you. *(long beat — hiss — she is deciding whether to say it)* I called to hear Mom laughing. That's — you know that's the only place her laugh still is? The end of your greeting. Where she's telling you it's still recording and you can't find the button. Sixteen years and you never fixed it, and Dad — *(steadies)* — I know why. Okay. *(beat)* I'm going to hang up and call it one more time, and I'm not going to say anything, so don't — *(catches it, almost laughs)* — so don't pick up. *(beat)* Bye, Dad.

*BEEP.*

---

CODA · THE GREETING, ONCE MORE — wear now 8

*Ring. Ring. The machine picks up: relay clunk, motor whir. The greeting plays a final time, worse than we have ever heard it — the pitch sags, words drop out. The piano under it, barely.*

WALTER: *(through wear)* Hello. You have reached the home of Walter Hargrove —

JUNE: And June.

WALTER: — and June Hargrove, who was supposed to wait for the light... kindly leave your name... it says tone here, but it's a beep.

JUNE: *(the laugh — it survives the wear)* Walter, it's still going —

WALTER: How do you stop the —

*The tape fails mid-word — on the question the machine spent sixteen years not answering. Not a beep: pitch collapse, transport stop, then nothing. Two seconds of true silence — the only silence in the piece with no hiss under it.*

AFTERWORD (for the site page / description — not performed)

Answering machines held the only recordings most families ever made of their own voices. Nobody spoke to them like they mattered. Every message on this tape was ephemera to the person leaving it — a casserole dish, a ride time, a kid's museum trip — and the machine kept them anyway, because keeping was all it knew how to do. The tape's wear pattern is the only narrator: nothing on it tells you June died; you know because message three is nearly worn through.

This is fiction. The Hargroves aren't real. The machine, in every family, was.
